Your phone rings. You look at the number and think, "Oh no, is it the bank again?" Forty calls, fifty calls in a day. Then the WhatsApp messages start. "Pay today." "Our person is coming to your house." "We'll file a police case." After a point, even the ringtone makes you tense, doesn't it? You are sitting with your family, but your mind is somewhere else. "What if they call my office? What if my family finds out?" Even at night, these thoughts keep coming back. If this sounds like your situation, take a moment. Take a deep breath. Let's talk about it calmly. When you can't pay your credit card bill, what can the bank actually do? What are your rights? How do you handle the calls? Can you ask for a settlement? And later, can you improve your credit score? We'll go through all of this, one step at a time. You don't need to know difficult banking words. I'll explain them as we go. First, one thing must be clear. If you have the money to pay, paying on time is better. This video is for people who are trying to pay but cannot manage it right now. Maybe your salary has stopped. Maybe someone at home needed hospital treatment. Maybe you are using one card to pay another, and now the bills have become too much. Saying, "I have money, but I'll not pay," and saying, "I want to pay, but I can't manage it now," are different situations. So first, be honest with yourself. How much can you really afford? Usually, the problem starts small. A hospital bill comes. A job is lost. A customer does not pay you on time. You think, "Let me use the card now. Next month, I'll clear it." But next month, another expense comes. So you arrange money from another card. Then you pay only the minimum due. After that, a small loan. Then a loan from an app. Your salary comes, and within a few days, almost everything is gone. You sit there wondering, "I am earning money. Then why am I always short?" Does that sound familiar? Let's stop and ask one question. If we keep taking new loans to pay old loans, is the total debt coming down? Or are we only moving the problem to next month? It is not an easy question. But answering it honestly helps you see what needs to change. And please remember this. Having debt does not make you a bad person. Being late with a bill does not decide your value. You have a money problem. A serious one, yes. But it needs a plan, support, and the right information. Shame will not pay the bill. And panic will not help you choose well. So, where do you start? Start with the needs of your home. Food. Rent. Medicines. Your children's school expenses. The basic things your family needs. Imagine paying someone just to stop the calls, and then having no money left for medicines. Will that plan work? First, write down your monthly income. Then write down these necessary expenses. After that, see how much is actually left for repayment. Put it on paper or in your phone. Don't keep doing the maths in your head. When you can see the numbers, you can make a proper decision. "Somehow, I'll manage" sounds hopeful. But knowing what you can afford is much more useful. Now, let us come to the biggest fear. "If I can't pay my credit card bill, will the police arrest me?" When someone cannot pay because of genuine money problems, the issue is generally about recovering the money through civil law. Missing a payment, by itself, doesn't automatically mean cheating. A cheating case depends on the facts. Was there a dishonest plan from the beginning? Were fake documents used? Was someone deliberately misled? Those are separate questions. The Supreme Court has also explained that breaking a payment agreement and cheating are not automatically the same thing. So a caller shouting, "We'll put a criminal case on you," does not decide what the law says. But please don't take this to mean, "Nothing can happen, so I can ignore everything." The bank can send a legal notice. It can start a recovery case. It can use the legal process that applies to your account.

And a court order can have serious consequences. If you receive a real legal notice, court summons, or arbitration notice, check it and respond properly. If you need help, speak to a lawyer or ask about legal aid. What matters is the actual document and the actual case. A loud voice on the phone is not proof. Now you may ask, "What about a cheque that bounced? Or an auto-debit that failed?" These can raise separate legal issues. The answer depends on the payment, the instructions you gave, the notice, and the facts. Don't assume that no other law can apply just because the original bill was from a credit card. Read the notice. Check what it says. Get advice when needed. Another common worry is, "I have dues with four or five banks. How do I handle all of them?" One call ends, and another starts. Sometimes, you can't even remember the exact balance on each card. So make one simple list. For each card, write the bank's name, the amount due, the due date, and your last payment date. Add any complaint number or legal notice you have received. Keep each account's papers together. That way, you are looking at facts instead of trying to remember everything while someone is pressuring you. Also, settling one card does not settle the others. Each account needs its own attention. And don't assume, "My amount is small, so this bank will never go to court." The bank's decision can depend on the account, its own policy, and the legal options available. Build your plan around checking and answering real communication. Don't build it around guesses about what the bank will or will not do. Now, open your statement. Have you ever thought, "I stopped using the card. They even blocked it. Then why is the balance still increasing?" Blocking the card may stop new purchases. It doesn't automatically stop interest or other charges that apply to the old balance. So don't look only at the final total. Check what is inside it. How much did you actually spend? How much is interest? How much is the late payment fee? What are the taxes? And where did your earlier payments go? Ask for a clear breakdown if you don't understand. Let's take an example. Suppose you owe one lakh rupees. If the monthly interest rate is three percent, that works out to about three thousand rupees in interest. This is just an example. Your card's actual rate and calculation can be different. Check your own terms. Now add any fees and taxes that apply. If your payment is small, very little may go towards the amount you originally spent. That is why you may feel, "I keep paying, but the bill is not coming down." Let's also clear up the minimum due. Paying that small amount does not clear your full bill. Interest may continue on the balance left unpaid. Depending on your card's terms, you may also lose the interest-free benefit. But the minimum due is not useless either. Paying the required minimum on time can help stop the account from becoming overdue. The difficulty comes when you keep paying only that amount, month after month. The debt can stay with you for a long time. So, if you can afford the full bill, clear it. If you can't, ask the bank for a repayment option you can manage. There is an important RBI protection here too. Unpaid taxes, levies, and charges must not be added to the principal just to charge or compound interest on them. In simple words, these unpaid amounts cannot simply become new borrowing on which the bank keeps charging interest. Also, when checking whether you crossed your credit limit for an over-limit fee, the bank must not include interest, taxes, and other charges in that calculation. If something looks wrong in your statement, ask the bank to explain it in writing. You don't have to accept a charge only because it appears on the bill. Now, does this mean the bank must remove all your interest and fees if you ask for settlement? No. The bank may agree to reduce some dues, but that is not guaranteed.

It depends on your situation, the account, and the bank's approval. Before asking for a settlement, understand the amount you spent, your payments, and the charges. Then you know what you are discussing. Next, check your salary account. Is it with the same bank where your credit card payment is overdue? If yes, read the account and card terms carefully. Look for words like "lien" or "set-off". These may sound confusing. The point is this: under the terms and law that apply, the bank may be able to use money in your account with it to pay your dues. So don't assume, "The card is separate from my savings account. My salary cannot be touched." Some agreements give the bank these rights. Think ahead about the money you need for rent, food, and medicines. If needed, get advice about your salary banking arrangement. But moving your salary to another account does not make the debt disappear or protect you from every legal recovery step. You still have to follow valid legal duties and court orders. The idea here is to understand the risk and plan for your family's basic needs. Now, how do you talk to the bank? After so many calls, you may think, "Let me switch off my phone. I just want some peace." That feeling is understandable. But the bank still needs to hear what your situation is. Instead of only handling calls, explain it in writing. Open the bank's official website. Find the grievance officer's or nodal officer's contact details. These are the bank's complaint contacts. Send an email explaining why payment has become difficult. You don't need big words. You can say, "My income has come down. I have medical expenses at home. I want to discuss a payment plan. After my basic expenses, this is what I can afford each month." Simple and honest is enough. Don't agree to an amount just to end the call. Saying, "I'll pay tomorrow," may give you a few minutes of peace. But if the money will not be there tomorrow, the same problem comes back. You can calmly say, "I can't promise that amount. Please tell me what other options are available." Ask about a smaller EMI, a payment plan, a change in repayment terms, or settlement. Ask which options your account qualifies for, and get the details in writing. You can ask them to contact you mainly by email. But that request doesn't automatically stop the bank from making lawful calls. Your preference for email and your right to complain about harassment are separate things. Save the emails, replies, attachments, and complaint numbers. Keep them in one folder. Will sending an email automatically stop a legal case? No. But it gives you a record. It shows what you told the bank, what you asked for, and how you tried to deal with the problem. Now, let us talk about recovery agents. They can contact you about payment. But that does not give them permission to insult you, threaten you, shame you in public, or disturb your family's privacy. RBI instructions don't allow threatening or anonymous calls, abusive messages, or repeated harassment. Calls for recovering overdue dues must not be made before eight in the morning or after seven in the evening. And no, that does not mean they can say anything they like between eight and seven. The time rule is one rule. Harassment is still not allowed. Suppose someone says, "We'll tell your neighbours. We'll come to your office and embarrass you. We'll trouble your family." You don't have to treat this as normal recovery. Ask calmly, "What is your name? Which agency are you from? Which bank are you calling for? Please send your authorisation details through an official email." You don't have to match their volume. The aim is to keep a record and deal with the issue through the right channel. You can say, "I have already explained my problem to the bank in writing. I'll discuss payment through the official channel. Please don't threaten me." Keep it short. You don't need to win a shouting match. What if someone on WhatsApp asks for your live location? Wait.

Do you know who this person is? Have you checked that the bank has authorised them? Don't send your location just because a stranger asks. Never share your OTP, PIN, password, or card details. A location request alone does not prove a person is fake. But you should check their identity before sharing personal information. Another message says, "We came to your house, but the door was locked." First, check. Who came? When? From which agency? Confirm through the bank's official contact. Some messages may be real. Some may not. Fear should not make the decision for you. And what about, "Click this link to see your legal notice"? Don't rush to tap an unknown link. Open the official bank app or website yourself, or call verified customer care. A legal message can arrive by email or WhatsApp too, so don't reject it only because of how it arrived. Check who sent it, the case details, and any court or lawyer information. Taking a few minutes to verify is better than clicking in panic. What if an agent actually comes to your house? First, stay calm. If you are uncomfortable, you don't have to invite them inside. Ask for their ID and the bank's authorisation letter. Check which account they are visiting about. Speak in a place where you feel safe. If you need support, ask someone you trust to stay with you. Suppose they say, "Sign this now. Give me cash. Hand over your phone." Don't act just because they are pressuring you. Read any paper before signing. If you don't understand it, get advice first. If there is force, a physical threat, or immediate danger, call one one two. Your safety comes first. At the same time, a recovery visit by an authorised person is not automatically illegal. What matters is how they behave and whether they follow the proper process. If someone is harassing you, how do you complain? Keep the dates, times, phone numbers, and messages. Save screenshots and any evidence you have. Instead of only saying, "They keep calling," explain what happened. "This number called on this date, at this time, and said these words." That makes your complaint clearer. Send it to the bank's grievance team and ask for a complaint number. If the bank rejects the complaint, gives an unsatisfactory reply, or does not reply within thirty days, you may be able to take it to the RBI Ombudsman under the rules that apply. This is RBI's process for looking into eligible complaints against banks. The official Complaint Management System address is cms dot rbi dot org dot in. One thing to remember: a complaint about harassment deals with how recovery is being done. It doesn't cancel what you owe. It also does not guarantee a settlement discount. But you can ask for fair treatment. Now, what if constant calls are disturbing your whole day? Use your phone settings. Block known abusive numbers. Turn on spam filters. WhatsApp also has a Silence Unknown Callers option. But keep a way for the bank to contact you properly. Check verified emails and collect official letters. Also think about your daily needs. If you silence every unknown caller, you may miss a hospital, job, or delivery call too. Choose what works for you. Set aside a regular time to check bank messages and reply when needed. You don't have to spend your whole day reacting to every ring. Will the account close if you stop answering for sixty days? There is no such guarantee. One recovery agency may stop calling, and another may start. The team may change. The debt does not disappear because the calls become quiet. Keep working on your budget, written communication, and repayment options. Now, let us understand the payment timeline. Your statement has a due date. Pay the full bill or the required minimum by that date. If the full bill is not paid, interest may apply. If even the full minimum due is not paid, the account becomes overdue. RBI rules say the account must be past due for more than three days before late payment charges can be added or it can be reported as past due.

But the delay is counted from the original due date. Those three days are not a new due date. Don't plan your payment around them. As the delay grows, recovery calls may increase, the bank may restrict the account, and your credit report may be affected. Some people think, "Nothing will happen until ninety days." That is not correct. Charges, overdue reporting, and recovery follow-up can happen earlier. Under RBI rules, if the statement's minimum amount due is not fully paid within ninety days from the payment due date, the credit card account is treated as an NPA. What is NPA? It means Non-Performing Asset. In simple words, the bank puts the account in a category for payments that have not come in as required. The bank then follows the accounting rules for that category. Does it mean you no longer have to pay? No. The name of the category does not cancel the debt. What if the bank offers to turn your balance into EMIs? Before saying yes, check the monthly payment, interest rate, number of months, any processing fee, and the total you will pay. Then ask yourself, "Can I manage this EMI every month?" If it is affordable, it may help. If it is too high, agreeing just to stop today's pressure may bring the same problem back next month. Now comes another confusing word: write-off. You hear it and think, "Finally, the bank has removed my debt!" Please check what they mean. A technical write-off is generally a change in the bank's accounts. It doesn't automatically mean the bank has forgiven your debt. Recovery can still continue. There is also no single rule saying every credit card must be written off after one hundred and eighty days. The timing can depend on the bank and the account. Waiting for write-off does not guarantee a big discount. If you are struggling now, you can ask the bank about workable options now. You don't have to wait for write-off before asking about settlement. Also, every bank can handle accounts differently. SBI Card, HDFC, ICICI, Axis, YES Bank, Kotak, RBL, and IDFC FIRST may have different processes. Even two people with the same bank may get different offers. Your friend says, "I settled by paying only twenty-five percent." That does not mean you will get the same offer. The age of the account, the balance, the person's finances, and the bank's approval can be different. Check your own written offer. Don't treat an online percentage as a promise. And don't judge an offer only by the caller's tone. A friendly person can still give you wrong information. A rude person is not a reason to ignore real account details. Verify first. So, what is settlement? The bank agrees to accept an agreed amount that is less than its total claim, and to waive the rest under agreed conditions. Both sides have to agree. Sending your request alone does not make it a settlement. The bank must approve it. You can say, "This is what I can arrange now. I can't promise more. Please consider a full and final settlement." Keep the amount and deadline realistic. If you take another very costly loan to pay the settlement, have you really solved the problem? You may have only changed who you owe. Imagine your balance is one lakh rupees. A caller says, "Pay thirty thousand rupees, and we'll close everything." You feel relieved, right? But before paying, ask for the official settlement letter. Check the offer through the bank's verified customer care. A low amount is useful only if the offer is real. The letter should clearly show your name, account, settlement amount, payment dates, and any instalment schedule. What happens to the remaining dues after you pay the agreed amount fully and on time? Will anything else be claimed? How will the account appear in your credit report? Understand these points before paying. Use an official bank channel or a letter whose authorisation the bank has confirmed. A WhatsApp screenshot alone is not enough.

If someone says, "Pay a little first. We'll send the letter later," ask what that payment will count as. Is it part of the approved settlement, or just an ordinary payment towards the balance? You should not discover later that the bank recorded it differently from what you expected. Pay only through a method the bank has authorised. Use its official app, website, or another confirmed payment channel. Don't send money to an agent's personal UPI account or hand over cash. Keep the receipt. If there are instalments, follow the dates carefully. Ask beforehand what happens if you miss one. After completing the payments, get written confirmation and the applicable No Dues or settlement completion certificate. If a court case is already running, confirm how it will be closed. "Don't worry, we'll handle it" on a phone call is not enough. Now, what about Lok Adalat? Hearing the name may make you nervous. "Do I have to go to court? What will they ask me?" A regular Lok Adalat gives both sides a chance to agree on a settlement. Eligible disputes can reach it before a case is filed or while a case is pending. Listen to the offer. Read the terms. See whether you can afford them. You don't have to accept every offer just because you went there. Agreement from both sides matters. But once an agreed settlement becomes a Lok Adalat award, it is final and binding, like a civil court decree. There is no ordinary appeal. So don't sign without understanding the amount, dates, and consequences. Ask, "After I pay this, will anything remain due? What happens if I miss a payment? What will my credit report show?" These are sensible questions. You have every reason to ask them. After settlement, another worry comes. "What about my CIBIL score? Will I ever get a loan again?" Settlement can affect future approvals. But don't assume your financial life is over. First, understand two words: Settled and Closed. Settled usually means the lender agreed to accept less than the full amount due. Closed is a different status, usually linked to full repayment. A lender may view Settled negatively when checking a new application. Getting a No Dues Certificate doesn't automatically mean your report will say Closed. The settlement being completed and the credit report status are different things. Can you improve your credit again? Yes, but there is no instant reset. First, make your income and household budget more stable. Try to build a small emergency fund. You don't need to apply for another card as soon as the old debt is settled. Later, if it suits your situation, you can look at an FD-backed secured card. This is a credit card supported by a fixed deposit. The bank decides whether to approve it, how much deposit is needed, and the limit. Making an FD does not guarantee approval. And don't lock away all your emergency money just to get a card. If you get one, use it for small expenses you have already planned. Pay the full bill by the due date. Keep your use low and affordable. Using ten or twenty percent of the limit may be one manageable approach, but no exact percentage guarantees a higher score. What matters most? Regular payments on time. Spending within your means. Avoiding repeated applications you don't need. And checking your credit report. After settlement, see whether the bank has updated it. If the balance or overdue status is wrong, or an account appears twice, raise a dispute with the bank and credit bureau. But correct negative history cannot be removed just because you want it gone. And please be careful with promises like, "Your score will reach seven hundred and fifty in eighteen months," or, "Everything will disappear in twenty-four months." Nobody can promise that for every person. Your history, current accounts, payments, and reporting all matter. Focus on habits you can keep following. One difficult period does not decide your whole life. If you feel scared or don't know how to tell your family, start with someone you trust.

A family member you feel safe talking to. A close friend. You don't have to carry the whole problem alone. You can say, "I am having money problems. These are my dues. This is my income. This is the plan I want to follow. I need some support." Asking for support is a useful step. Now, let us put everything into eight simple steps. Step one: list every card and balance. Write down your income, basic expenses, and repayment money. Check the terms and any risk to your salary account. Step two: explain your difficulty through each bank's official complaint channel. Ask for payment options you can afford. Save the replies and complaint numbers. Step three: manage the calls with your phone settings. But keep checking verified bank emails, letters, and genuine legal notices. Step four: look carefully at taking new loans to pay old ones. Understand what minimum payments do and cost. Don't promise more than your budget allows. Step five: save evidence of threats or harassment and complain. Use the RBI complaint process when appropriate. If you are in immediate danger, call one one two. Step six: remember that NPA, write-off, and settlement mean different things. Write-off does not cancel your debt. Ask about workable options. Step seven: verify any settlement letter. Check the amount, dates, remaining-dues waiver, and credit reporting. Pay through an authorised method and collect receipts and completion confirmation. Step eight: make your income more stable, build emergency savings, and check your credit report. Later, use suitable credit carefully and pay fully on time. Now ask yourself, "What can I do today?"
